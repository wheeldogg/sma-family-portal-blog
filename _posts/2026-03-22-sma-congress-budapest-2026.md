*By Shane Whelan — March 2026*

---

Last week I was in Budapest for the 5th International Scientific Congress on SMA, organised by SMA Europe. I went as a dad — Theo's dad, specifically. He's five, he has SMA Type 1, and he received Zolgensma as a baby. I also went as someone who builds things for a living (I'm a software engineer), which means I tend to look at systems — how they work, where they break, and what it would take to fix them. What I saw in Budapest gave me a lot to think about on all of those fronts.

This post is my attempt to share the key takeaways with other Irish SMA families. It's not a medical paper and it's not a formal conference report. It's what I learned, what surprised me, and what I think we can do about it.

## The Big Picture: Dual Therapy Is Happening — Just Not Here

The single biggest message from Budapest is this: **combination therapy for SMA — giving a second treatment after Zolgensma — is no longer theoretical. It's happening in clinical practice across Europe.**

France has approved risdiplam as an add-on therapy for children who've already received Zolgensma. Their neuromuscular centres are actively prescribing it. Germany is doing it on a case-by-case basis. In the United States, a 2023 Cure SMA survey found that over 12% of SMA patients are already on combination therapy in real-world practice.

And then there's Ireland.

Ireland's HSE Managed Access Protocol currently restricts risdiplam to monotherapy. If your child received Zolgensma, they can only access risdiplam if they meet "non-success" criteria — essentially, they need to show documented decline before they qualify for additional treatment. The framework was written before most of the current evidence existed.

Let me be blunt: we are asking our children to get worse before we'll give them more help. That doesn't sit right with me, and after Budapest, I'm more certain than ever that the evidence doesn't support it.

## The Evidence Is Clear

Several key studies were presented and discussed at the congress. Here's what stood out:

**The RESPOND trial** (published in the *Journal of Clinical Investigation*, September 2025) showed that 95% of children who received nusinersen after Zolgensma had motor improvements. Neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels — a biomarker for nerve damage — dropped 70–78%, confirming that a second therapy provides real, measurable biological benefit.

**The Stanford presymptomatic cohort** (presented at MDA 2025) followed 8 infants who received Zolgensma within the first two months of life — the best possible timing. Every single one of them eventually needed additional therapy. And every single one improved after receiving it.

**A US multicentre case series** (MDA 2026) tracked 19 patients across 6 centres who received risdiplam after Zolgensma. All 14 who were assessed for motor function showed improvement.

**A systematic review** (Bemanalizadeh et al., *European Journal of Pediatrics*, 2025) looked at 19 published studies and 6 ongoing trials on combination therapy. The finding that matters most to families: no additive toxicity signals in any combination. The treatments appear safe together.

And risdiplam is now on the **WHO Essential Medicines List** — one of the most broadly endorsed treatments in the world. Restricting access to a WHO essential medicine from children who've already had gene therapy is increasingly difficult to defend.

## What I Saw and Heard in Budapest

Beyond the data, the congress was full of moments that will stay with me.

I spoke with French neuromuscular specialists who confirmed that dual therapy is standard practice in their centres for children showing plateau or suboptimal response after Zolgensma. This isn't experimental for them. It's clinical reality.

I connected with SMA UK, who shared their survey data on how disease-modifying therapies are changing outcomes. I met representatives from SMA Italy, whose comprehensive family support model — including a 24-hour helpline staffed by physios, doctors, attorneys, and peer counsellors — puts most other countries to shame. Their "team over the individual" philosophy and dedicated school support programme are things Ireland could learn from.

I also attended practical workshops that had immediate takeaways for our daily life:

- **Cough assist protocols** — specific techniques including Ambu bag methods and pressure settings (35+/35-) that I hadn't encountered before
- **Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES)** — worth discussing with your child's physiotherapy team
- **Guitar as fine motor therapy** — particularly relevant for powered wheelchair users
- **Adapted sports** — iKarate (adapted martial arts) and boccia as competitive, inclusive options
- **Finger lights** — a simple sensory and motor play tool for younger children

These are the kinds of practical insights that make a real difference in day-to-day family life with SMA.

## New Science on the Horizon

Two areas of emerging research caught my attention.

### Metabolomics and SMA

New research is connecting SMA to glucose and lipid metabolism — suggesting the disease affects more than just motor neurons. It reaches into whole-body metabolic function. This could explain the fatigue and energy issues that so many SMA patients experience daily, and might eventually lead to new biomarkers for monitoring treatment response and even metabolic interventions alongside existing treatments.

### Apitegromab — A Potential Fourth Treatment

Scholar Rock presented data on apitegromab, a selective anti-myostatin antibody. This is a muscle-targeted therapy that works through a completely different mechanism than existing SMN-enhancing treatments like Zolgensma, risdiplam, or nusinersen. The Phase 3 SAPPHIRE trial met its primary endpoint in October 2024, a US FDA filing is under review, and European filing is underway. If approved, it could be added on top of any existing treatment to provide additional muscle-level benefit.

### Fatigue Research

The SMA Effort study and research into proximal predominant fatigue are examining the lived experience of SMA beyond standard motor function scores. Many SMA patients experience significant fatigue that isn't captured by clinical assessments like CHOP-INTEND or HFMSE. This research validates what families already know: our children get tired in ways the numbers don't always reflect.

## What Can Families Do?

I came home from Budapest feeling something I hadn't felt in a while: agency. Not because someone handed me a solution, but because I could see the paths more clearly. Here's what I think Irish SMA families can do:

### 1. Talk to Your Clinical Team About Dual Therapy

If your child received Zolgensma and you believe they could benefit from additional treatment, raise it with your consultant. Ask about the current evidence, ask about NfL testing, and ask what the process would be to apply through Named Patient access or an individual patient application.

### 2. Know Your Access Routes

There are pathways that exist right now:
- **Named Patient access** — your treating consultant can apply directly
- **EU Cross-Border Healthcare Directive** — allows consultation with specialist centres in other EU countries
- **EURO-NMD network** — European Reference Network for neuromuscular diseases, which can provide expert opinions

### 3. Connect with Other Families

If you're in a similar position — a child who's had Zolgensma and you're looking at what's next — you're not alone. Coordinated advocacy from multiple families is far more powerful than individual requests. Reach out to SMA Ireland or connect with other parents through the community.

### 4. Push for a Review of the MAP Criteria

The HSE's Managed Access Protocol for risdiplam was written in a different evidence landscape. The criteria that exclude post-Zolgensma children from risdiplam access need to be reviewed in light of the studies published in 2025 and 2026. SMA Ireland can and should advocate for this formally.

### 5. Stay Informed

The science is moving fast. New treatments, new evidence, new access decisions — they're happening quarterly now, not annually. Follow SMA Europe, Cure SMA, and SMA UK for the latest updates.

## Looking Forward

I came back from Budapest tired, slightly overwhelmed, and cautiously hopeful. The evidence for combination therapy is strong and growing. The clinical practice in other European countries has moved ahead. Ireland needs to catch up, and I believe we have the tools and the community to make it happen.

Theo is five. He's funny, stubborn, obsessed with Bluey, and has opinions about everything. He deserves every chance at the best possible outcome, and so does every other child with SMA in Ireland. The science says dual therapy works. The rest of Europe is acting on it. It's time we did too.

If you want to talk about any of this, or if you're navigating the same questions with your own family, please reach out. I'm happy to share what I've learned.
